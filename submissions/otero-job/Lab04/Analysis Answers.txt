Analysis Answers

1. How does error change as angle increases?
For a fixed N, error grows sharply with angle — at N=1, error rises from 0.005% (1°) to 4.72% (30°), nearly a 1000× increase. This is because the Maclaurin series is a Taylor expansion around θ=0; the truncation error scales with θ^(2N+1), so larger angles push you further from the expansion point and each omitted term contributes more.

2. How does error change as more terms are added?
Error shrinks dramatically — roughly 2-4 orders of magnitude per added term (e.g., at 30°: 4.72% → 0.065% → 0.0004% → ~0% going from N=1 to N=4). This matches the θ^(2N+1)/(2N+1)! factor: each new term adds a higher power of θ divided by a fast-growing factorial, so the correction shrinks fast, especially once (2N+1)! outpaces θ^(2N+1).

3. For small angles (< 5°), how many terms are sufficient?
Just 1 term. At 1°, 2°, and 5°, the N=1 error is already ≤0.127%, which is negligible for most engineering purposes. This is the classic "small-angle approximation" sin(θ) ≈ θ — valid because higher-order terms are suppressed by θ³, θ⁵, etc., which are tiny when θ is small.

4. For larger angles (> 20°), how many terms are needed?
At least 3 terms to get well below 0.1% error (N=3 gives 0.0004% at 30°). N=2 is borderline (0.065% at 30°) — acceptable for rough engineering estimates but not high-precision work. N=1 is clearly inadequate (4.72% error at 30°).

Taylor Series

Which performs better near 10°? Taylor-at-10°, decisively — it's exact at 10° (zero error, since θ−a=0 there) and stays far more accurate than Maclaurin for every angle from 10° to 30° in this range.

Which is better far from 10°? Maclaurin — at 1°, 2°, and 5° (all far from the Taylor center but close to 0), Maclaurin's error is 2–4 orders of magnitude smaller.

Why: each series' accuracy is governed by distance from its own center — error scales with (θ − center)^(N+1). Maclaurin is centered at 0, so it's most accurate near 0° and degrades as θ grows. Taylor-at-10° is centered at 10°, so it's most accurate near 10° and degrades as you move away in either direction (note the errors grow both below and above 10° in the second table). The crossover in this data lands right around 10°, exactly where you'd expect.

Analysis Answers

1. Does adding more terms always improve the approximation?
Yes, for a fixed center and a fixed θ — each added term is the next-order correction, so error monotonically shrinks (this is guaranteed for sin/cos since the series converges for all θ). What does not improve monotonically is comparing across different centers: Taylor@10° can need more terms than Maclaurin even though it's a "better" expansion point for angles near 10°, simply because Maclaurin's odd-only terms reach higher polynomial degree per term (as seen earlier).

2. Why is Maclaurin most accurate near zero?
It's a Taylor series centered at a=0, so the error term scales with θ^(N+1). The smaller θ is, the faster that power shrinks toward zero — accuracy degrades as you move away from the center, in either direction.

3. Why can changing the Taylor expansion point improve accuracy?
Because the error scales with (θ − a)^(N+1), not θ^(N+1). If you know in advance that you only care about angles near some value (say 10°), centering there makes (θ − a) small for your region of interest even though θ itself isn't small — trading global accuracy near 0 for local accuracy near a.

4. How does distance from the expansion point affect accuracy?
Error grows with |θ − a| raised to the power of (number of terms + 1) — so accuracy is best right at the center and degrades polynomially (then eventually the factorial in the denominator wins) as you move away. This is why Task 1 shows Taylor@10° needing more terms as you move from 10° toward 1° or 30°.

5. What is the critical angle for sin(θ) ≈ θ?
≈4.4°–4.5° for 0.1% tolerance. Below this, the one-term small-angle approximation is good enough; above it, the θ³/6 correction term becomes necessary.
